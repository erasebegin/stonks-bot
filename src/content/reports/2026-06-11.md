## 📚 Learning Corner: The P/E Ratio — Cheap vs. Expensive

Today's report is a perfect case study in why **P/E ratios alone don't tell the full story.**

Look at this snapshot from today's data:

| Ticker | P/E | Day Change |
|--------|-----|-----------|
| TSLA | 370.5 | -3.80% |
| AMD | 151.3 | -4.86% |
| PLTR | 146.3 | -1.41% |
| MRVL | 87.1 | -5.35% |
| AVGO | 61.7 | -5.12% |
| ASML | 59.5 | -2.45% |
| MSFT | 23.7 | -1.50% |
| INFY | 14.7 | -1.85% |
| AA | 16.8 | -9.47% |

You'd think the lowest P/E stocks would be the safest (AA at 16.8, INFY at 14.7). But AA dropped 9.47%! Meanwhile PLTR at 146.3 P/E only fell 1.41%.

**Why? Because P/E is backward-looking and context-dependent.**

- **AA (Alcoa)** is a commodity cyclical — low P/E because earnings are inflated by a cyclical upswing. When Iran tensions rise, commodity stocks get repriced on fear of demand destruction.
- **PLTR (Palantir)** has a high P/E because the market is pricing in future growth. The stock held better because their government contracts provide revenue stability regardless of economic conditions.
- **MSFT (23.7 P/E)** is arguably the best value in tech — real earnings, real growth, real moat, reasonable multiple.

**The takeaway:** Low P/E doesn't mean "cheap" if earnings are about to fall. High P/E doesn't mean "expensive" if growth is durable. Always ask: *are today's earnings sustainable?*
